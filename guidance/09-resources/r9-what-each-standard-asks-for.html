This playbook is based on three documents: GovS 010, the AQuA Book and the Code of Practice for Statistics. What each one requires is below, with the AQuA Book’s RIGOUR principles in full. Which standards apply to you says which of them applies to your work, and when.

GovS 010: Analysis

The government functional standard for analysis. It sets out five principles and the governance and accountabilities for analysis in an organisation. It also covers the analytical cycle, which it sets out in five stages, and thirteen supporting practices. This playbook sets the same cycle out as five phases. Delivery, communication and sign-off are one phase, and review is added as the fifth.

The thirteen are: public interest; inputs and methods; supporting decision-making; uncertainty; analytical verification and validation; documentation and record keeping; quality assurance; collaborative working; learning from experience; analytical models; statistics; appraisal and evaluation; and personal data.1 Each has a section of this playbook that meets it, and where each standard is met says which.

When it applies: always. It applies to all analysis undertaken in government, whoever does it.

NHSBSA is assessed against GovS 010, and this playbook is part of how NHSBSA meets it.

What is binding

GovS 010 separates shall, a requirement, from should, a recommendation. Shall appears eleven times in the whole document. One of the eleven is in the conventions table that defines the term.

Seven of the remaining ten say that a different functional standard shall be followed: GovS 005 Digital, GovS 008 Commercial or GovS 011 Communication. That leaves three substantive requirements:

  1. The principles. The standard puts them as “those engaged in analysis shall ensure”.2 Five of the seven principles in this playbook are the standard’s own, and R19. Standards to principles mapping names the clause each one answers to.
  2. A senior officer accountable for analysis. GovS 010 requires NHSBSA to have a senior officer accountable for analysis as an activity.3
  3. Analytical roles and accountabilities. GovS 010 requires NHSBSA to define analytical roles in its governance and management framework, and to assign them to people who can hold them.4 The standard describes those people as having appropriate seniority, skills and experience.

The first applies to the analyst. The other two apply to the organisation, separately from the four roles you hold on a piece of work.

NHSBSA records who holds the other two in its governance and management framework, not in this playbook.

Everything else in the standard is a should, the thirteen practices included. GovS 010 calls a should advisory. The AQuA Book adds that a should is met on a “comply or explain” basis,5 so you can depart from a should only with a reason you can explain. Record any departure and its reason.

The sentence “All analysis shall be assured” is not from GovS 010. It comes from chapter 3 of the AQuA Book.6 The Analytical Assurance Decision Tool states it as an instruction.

The AQuA Book

Guidance on producing quality analysis for government. Where GovS 010 sets the requirement, the AQuA Book says how to meet it.

Where the AQuA Book and GovS 010 overlap, the playbook follows the AQuA Book. That rule is the playbook’s own.

Four things in this playbook come from it. The analytical lifecycle, which the AQuA Book sets out in four stages.7 The proportionality approach in deciding your assurance level, which is its chapter 3.6 Its four roles, which are the four in roles and responsibilities.8 And the RIGOUR principles.9

Chapter 3 also states two requirements. All analysis shall be assured, and the assurer and the analyst shall be independent.6 The AQuA Book states further requirements with shall for the assurer, the approver and the analyst, in sections 5.1,8 8.1.3,10 9.1.211 and 9.1.4.12

When it applies: always, as the practical companion to GovS 010.

Quality assurance and engagement in every phase

The AQuA Book says that quality assurance activities should take place throughout an analytical project.7 It also says that an effective quality assurance process involves ongoing engagement between the commissioner and the analyst.

The engagement is there to keep an appropriate balance between time, resource and quality. It is also there to build a shared understanding of the assurance activities required and the risks involved.

In this playbook, quality assurance is:

  • planned in Phase 1, engagement and scoping
  • designed in Phase 2, design
  • done in Phase 3, analysis
  • stated in Phase 4, delivery, communication and sign-off
  • tested in Phase 5, review

Customer and stakeholder engagement runs through all five phases in the same way. The commissioner has a part in every phase.

RIGOUR, in full

Repeatable. For an analytical process to be considered valid we might reasonably expect that the analysis produces the same outputs for the same inputs and constraints. Different analysts might approach the analytical problem in different ways, while methods might include randomised processes. In such cases, exact matches are not guaranteed or expected. Taking this into account, repeatability means that if an approach is repeated the results should be as expected.

Independent. Analysis should be (as far as possible) free of prejudice or bias. Care should be taken to balance views appropriately across all stakeholders and experts.

Grounded in reality. Quality analysis takes the Commissioner and Analyst on a journey as views and perceptions are challenged and connections are made between the analysis and its real consequences. Connecting with reality like this guards against failing to properly grasp the context of the problem that is being analysed.

Objective. Effective engagement and suitable challenge reduce the risk of bias and enables the Commissioner and the Analyst to be clear about the interpretation of results.

Uncertainty-managed. Uncertainty is identified, managed and communicated throughout the analytical process.

Robust. Analytical results are error free in the context of residual uncertainty and accepted limitations that make sure the analysis is used appropriately.9

Repeatable is not the same as automated. Reproducible and automated analysis compared sets out the difference.

Reproducible and automated analysis compared

Repeatable is the first letter of RIGOUR.9 It does not mean automated. Automation can help make analysis reproducible, but it is not enough on its own.

Reproducible and automated analysis both aim at efficiency and reliability. They work on different parts of the process. Each has its own question:

  • Reproducibility. Can someone else, or even you later on, given the same inputs, understand and recreate your work?
  • Automation. Can a computer run the analysis, with any valid inputs, with minimal or no human intervention?

Good practice is a fully reproducible analysis that includes a level of automation appropriate to the complexity and the effort of repeating it manually.

The two differ on six considerations.

Reproducible and automated analysis side by side, one consideration to a row
Consideration Reproducible analysis Automated analysis
Primary goal Verifiability, transparency, reusability Efficiency, scalability, speed
Main activities Documenting all steps and key decisions, managing versions Scripting, scheduling, pipelines, monitoring
Does it require automation? No Yes
Is it essential? Yes No, but desirable, especially as an analysis grows in complexity
Testing the analysis Use static input data, for known results Include tests that run automatically when the analysis changes
Consistency In results In processes

The Code of Practice for Statistics

Maintained by the UK Statistics Authority and regulated by the Office for Statistics Regulation. Edition 3.0 has been in force since 30 October 2025.

It keeps the three pillars of Trustworthiness, Quality and Value, and sets out ten Standards for Official Statistics beneath them.

Code of Practice for Statistics standards, their pillars and what producers must do
Standard Pillar Producers must
1. Show integrity Trustworthiness Act honestly, objectively and professionally, and challenge misuse
2. Lead responsibly Trustworthiness Make impartial decisions under the Head of Profession for Statistics
3. Be transparent Trustworthiness Release at a standard time, pre-announced
4. Manage data responsibly Trustworthiness Handle data safely and be open about its use
5. Prioritise quality Quality Support a quality culture that encourages learning and improvement
6. Be rigorous Quality Use suitable data and sound methods, and assure quality across production and release
7. Be open about quality Quality Explain strengths, limitations and uncertainty prominently
8. Be relevant Value Put users at the centre of decisions about the statistics
9. Be clear Value Present statistics to support appropriate interpretation
10. Be accessible Value Ensure statistics are accessible and freely available to all

When it applies: for official statistics, in full and as a regulatory requirement. NHSBSA official statistics are listed in the statistical collections.

You may apply the Code voluntarily

If you produce data, statistics or analysis that is not official statistics, you may still apply the Code to show a commitment to trustworthiness, quality and public value.13

Consider it for anything published or quoted externally. It is a choice, not an obligation, so make that choice deliberately.
